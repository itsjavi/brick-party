# Influences

What Brick Party's humor is modeled on, so you can judge whether a batch hits the register the user wants. People
laugh at different things, so the influences span several senses of humor, grouped below. This file is for the
coordinator. Writers get the mechanisms through [humor.md](humor.md); the names and sources below stay out of every
brief and every pack. Use what each influence teaches, never what it contains: its characters, catchphrases, gadgets,
episode premises and running gags have no place in the game.

Every influence also has a side the game's safety rules leave out (crude and gross-out jokes, body horror, innuendo,
religion, politics, darkness beyond cartoon peril), so take only the structural moves listed here.

## Chaotic absurd: *Bobobo-bo Bo-bobo* and *Rick and Morty*

The user set the bar for the extreme end of the game's absurdity at "a bobobo or rick-and-morty level of absurdness".
That's the `unhinged` style in `categories.ts`, and the punch that `dark` and `satire` items need when they're spicy.
Unhinged works at both spice levels: mild unhinged is harmless surreal nonsense, spicy unhinged adds an edge of
roast or doom. A request for "that level" sets the style, not the spice; keep the spice the brief settled.

***Bobobo-bo Bo-bobo*** (manga by Yoshio Sawai, anime from 2003) teaches:

- **Manzai rhythm.** A fool says something absurd and a straight man reacts, often both inside one line. A deadpan
  setup ("How satisfied are you with...") followed by a ridiculous subject is the same move.
- **Derailment.** A tense moment swerves into an unrelated skit and nobody acknowledges the swerve.
- **Solemn names for silly moves.** A trivial action gets announced like a forbidden martial-arts technique.
- **Ego in objects.** Food and household objects with enormous egos and grave melodrama about trivia.
- **Puns and double-talk**, and the occasional wink at the fact that this is a game.

***Rick and Morty*** (adult animated series, from 2013) teaches:

- **The one-gag commercial.** The "interdimensional cable" segments sell a nonsensical product or show with total
  sincerity, and each ends before the gag gets old.
- **Cosmic stakes, petty concerns.** Universe-ending events handled like paperwork, and paperwork handled like the
  end of the universe.
- **The trivial machine's purpose.** A gadget built for one tiny job realizes that's all it's for.
- **Infinite versions of you.** Parallel universes as a casual premise.
- **Nihilism played for laughs.** Existential dread treated as an ordinary Tuesday. Critics read its absurdism as
  characters chasing meaning in a meaningless universe: "humanly impossible" rather than "logically impossible".

## Rigorous absurd: *Monty Python* and *The Good Place*

Nonsense with airtight internal logic, for players who like their absurdity precise. Feeds `unhinged` and `absurd`.

***Monty Python*** (the British troupe's TV sketches from 1969, and films) teaches:

- **Rigorous nonsense.** Flawless reasoning applied to a ridiculous premise, followed all the way.
- **Denying the obvious.** A clerk or shopkeeper calmly insists the plainly false is true, in a customer-service voice.
- **The official department of something silly.** A ministry, committee or form for a trivial thing, run with total
  seriousness.
- **The swerve and the abrupt stop.** A sketch lurches into something completely different, or gets halted for being
  too silly.

***The Good Place*** (sitcom, 2016-2020) teaches:

- **Points for everything.** A cosmic system scores everyday acts with absurd precision, and nobody scores well.
- **The perfect place, badly run.** An ideal world turns out to be managed like a dysfunctional company, with
  committees, paperwork and loopholes.
- **Moral philosophy for trivia.** Grand ethical questions applied to tiny everyday choices.

Its afterlife frame touches religion, which the game leaves out: take the scoring system and the bureaucracy.

## Workplace cringe: *The Office* (US)

The awkward, recognizable side of work, for players who laugh at what's true. Feeds `observational` and `satire`.

***The Office*** (US sitcom, 2005-2013) teaches:

- **The self-image gap.** Someone's confident idea of themselves next to what everyone else plainly sees, like a boss
  desperate to be liked.
- **The straight reaction.** One deadpan look at the camera says everything; in text, a flat aside after the absurd
  line.
- **The petty ceremony.** Trivial office achievements and rituals treated with full pomp: awards, drills,
  traditions nobody wanted.
- **Mundane stakes, total commitment.** A stolen desk item or a seating change handled like a crisis.

## Institutions and the future: *The Simpsons* and *Futurama*

Satire of how things are run, now and centuries from now. Feeds `satire`, `wordplay` and the `ai` and `engineering`
topics.

***The Simpsons*** (animated sitcom, from 1989) teaches:

- **Every institution, slightly broken.** One small town holds a plant, a school, a newsroom and a city hall, each
  run a little absurdly.
- **The everyman against the institution.** A simple want meets a system that makes it impossible.
- **The sign gag.** A marquee, a label or a store name carries a quick pun or an understated joke in the background.
- **The boss who doesn't know your name**, however long you've worked there.

***Futurama*** (animated sci-fi sitcom, from 1999) teaches:

- **The far future, same job.** A thousand years of progress and work is still a delivery job with a bad boss.
- **Robots with human vices.** Machines that are lazy, vain, greedy or petty.
- **Today as an ancient relic.** Ordinary present-day things puzzled over by people in a distant future.
- **Future tech that fixes nothing.** Amazing inventions applied to the same old problems.

## The game formats

These name the formats the games borrow, and they're already in the code and the prompts: Jackbox (the party-game
shell), Cards Against Humanity (Worst Feedback Ever's fill-in-the-blank cards) and Gartic Phone (Skip Logic's
telephone game). Each game's mechanics in the content guide are what a writer needs.
